Your hand on the dial

Three essays of diagnosis: who emits, what your share is, what your summer becomes. This last one is the treatment room. Six dials control the world's emissions, the physics does the rest, and the temperature the century settles at is the one you choose. The machine is simple and honest about being simple; every number in it has a source you can check at the bottom.

Carbon budget arithmetic from IPCC AR6; emissions from the Global Carbon Budget 2025; benchmarks from Climate Action Tracker

The century so far

The green line is what has already happened: the world's CO₂ output, fossil fuels plus deforestation, climbing from 32 billion tonnes a year in 2000 to about 42 today. Everything the first three essays counted flows through this one line.

Hands off

The dashed line is the century on autopilot: policies as they stand, emissions flat for decades, then drifting down as old plants retire. Follow it to 2100 and the readout lands near 2.7°C above the preindustrial world, which is where the Climate Action Tracker's current-policies estimate has sat, give or take a tenth, for four years.

The physics in one line

The engine under the readout is the same arithmetic the IPCC uses for carbon budgets: every 1,000 billion tonnes of CO₂ warms the planet by about 0.45°C, likely between 0.27 and 0.63. That range is the honest blur on every number this page shows you. The warming we already have, 1.37°C, is the starting point; on present course the 1.5 mark falls around 2030.

Dial one: coal

Coal-fired electricity is the single biggest block: about 10 billion tonnes of CO₂ a year. Phasing it out is the one lever every credible pathway pulls first. When does the last unabated coal plant close?

Dial two: the roads

Road transport, cars and trucks together, is about 6 billion tonnes, three quarters of all transport. The dial here is the year new petrol and diesel sales end; the fleet takes another fifteen years to turn over. Aviation and shipping stay in the baseline, this dial does not touch them.

Dial three: the forests

Deforestation still adds about 4 billion tonnes a year, mostly in the tropics. Unlike a power plant, stopping it requires no new technology at all, only that standing forests stay standing.

Dial four: methane

Methane is not CO₂, so it gets its own physics: cut it and the planet cools within decades, not centuries. The UN's methane assessment prices a 45% cut by 2030, plugged leaks, flared wells, changed farming, at nearly 0.3°C avoided by the 2040s. This dial moves the readout directly rather than bending the curve.

Dial five: everything else

Gas and oil power stations, cement kilns, steel mills, boilers, shipping, planes: roughly 20 billion tonnes that no single headline policy covers. This is the grind dial, a thousand efficiency standards and electrifications, and it is worth more than coal and cars combined.

Dial six: the vacuum

Carbon removal, machines and forests pulling CO₂ back out of the sky. Pathways that hold 1.5°C lean on it heavily. Be aware of what you are buying: today it operates at a tiny fraction of this scale, and nobody has proven it works at billions of tonnes a year. This is the speculative dial.

The reckoning

The scale

A scale of futures, from the 1.37°C we live at now to the mid-fours. The two black posts are the Paris agreement: hold well below 2, aim for 1.5. Every dial above moves a marker along this line.

What each stop costs

The stops are not evenly priced. At 1.5°C, 70 to 90% of coral reefs decline; at 2°C, over 99% are gone. Somewhere between 2 and 3 degrees, held for long enough, Greenland and West Antarctica pass a point of no return worth 9 to 11 metres of sea over the millennia that follow. By 2100 itself the likely sea rise runs from about 30 cm on the lowest pathway to a metre on the highest.

Whose future sits where

The world's three benchmark futures: current policies near 2.6°C, every pledge and target delivered near 2.2, every net-zero promise kept near 1.9. And in ember, the future you dialled in above. Scroll back up and move it.

What the dials are for

  • No single dial saves the century. Max out coal alone and the readout barely clears the pledges line. The futures below 2°C all require most dials turned most of the way, which is the honest answer to "what actually works": everything, together, starting now.
  • The dials are political, not personal. Part two ended where personal maths runs out, at the 2-tonne line no lifestyle reaches. These six levers are pulled in parliaments, boardrooms and trade deals, by people who answer, eventually, to readers of pages like this one.
  • Tenths are the unit of victory. Between 2.7°C and 1.9 there is no finish line to cross, only tenths of a degree, and part three showed what a tenth is worth in hot days and sleepless nights. Every dial position that shaves one off is a win that arrives whether or not the headline target is met.

The first essay asked who is heating the planet. Four essays later the answer has not changed, but it has an instrument panel.

Sources & methodology

The model is deliberately minimal. Warming in any year is 1.37°C (the human-induced level in 2025, per the Indicators of Global Climate Change update) plus 0.45°C per 1,000 GtCO₂ of cumulative emissions from 2026 (the IPCC AR6 transient climate response to cumulative emissions, likely range 0.27 to 0.63, shown wherever a headline number is shown), minus up to 0.3°C for methane action (UNEP/CCAC Global Methane Assessment). The baseline holds the Global Carbon Budget 2025 total of 42.2 GtCO₂ flat to 2050 then declines gently, a shape calibrated so that hands-off lands on the Climate Action Tracker current-policies estimate. Dials subtract each sector's present emissions along a phase-out ramp: coal power about 10 GtCO₂ (IEA), road transport about 6 (three quarters of 8 Gt transport, IEA via Our World in Data), deforestation 4.1 (GCB 2025), the remainder about 20. Known limits: the wedges subtract from an aggregate baseline, so where current policies already assume some sector decline a dial's late-century effect is slightly overstated; aerosol changes and carbon-cycle feedbacks are ignored, which makes the strongest-mitigation futures read about a tenth optimistic against IPCC pathway ensembles; carbon removal at 5 to 10 Gt a year is unproven. With every dial maxed the model peaks near 1.4°C and turns net negative in the 2080s, consistent with the IPCC's strongest-mitigation category.
