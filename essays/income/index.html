A data essay

Income:
perception vs reality

Ask people where they sit on the income ladder and almost everyone, rich or poor, reaches for the same word: average. This page is about the gap between where we feel we are and where the official numbers actually put us.

The distribution

Here is the whole country, lined up by income. Each bar is a slice of the population at a given weekly income, adjusted for household size and measured after housing costs. Most people are bunched to the left, with a long tail of high earners trailing off to the right.

The average income is £694 a week. It sounds like a reasonable middle. But it is not the middle: the long tail of high earners drags it upward, and roughly two in three households earn less than the average.

The real middle is the median: £562 a week, about £29,250 a year for a couple with no children. Half of Britain lives on less than this, half on more. When people picture "average", this is closer to the truth.

And the poverty line sits at 60% of that median: £338 a week. Everyone in the shaded band lives below it. That is 21% of the population, around one in five, after housing costs.

The instinct“I’m somewhere in the middle”

It is the most reliable finding in the study of how people see money. Asked to place themselves on the income ladder, people cluster near the middle, whether they are near the top or near the bottom. High earners feel typical. So do low earners. Almost nobody thinks they are rich, and almost nobody thinks they are poor.

So before reading on, find out where you actually stand. Enter your household's total take-home pay. The calculator adjusts it for your household size the same way the official statistics do, then places you against everyone else.

Surprised? Most people are. The number that felt middling is often near the top, because the yardstick in your head is your neighbours and your feed, not the country. The rest of this essay is about the reality that yardstick hides.

The toolDraw your own line

Pick a group, choose whether to count income before or after housing, and drag the threshold. The bar reads out how much of Britain lives below it. Notice how quickly the share climbs: a threshold that feels modest already sits above most of the country.

Myth“Most people are comfortable”

A common way to name the rungs, borrowed from the OECD, splits everyone by their distance from the median: in poverty below 60%, squeezed from 60 to 75%, middle or comfortable from 75 to 200%, and well-off above that.

Split the whole population and a reassuring majority lands in "comfortable".

But that band is enormous: it stretches from £422 to £1,125 a week. It holds the household one payslip from trouble and the household with a second home alike. "Comfortable" is a statistical category, not a feeling.

Split out children and the picture darkens. Nearly a third are below the poverty line, the highest rate of any age group.

Split out pensioners and, after housing, the poverty slice is the smallest of all. Hold that thought.

Myth“The old are the poor ones”

Pensioners are the face people reach for when they picture hardship. The measured picture is the reverse, and it turns on one thing: housing. Watch what happens when you take rent and mortgage interest out of the sum.

Before housing, pensioners look poorer than working-age adults. After housing, they overtake nobody: so many own their home outright that their costs fall away, while children, who mostly live in renting or mortgaged families, are pushed the other way. The same housing costs flip the ranking.

Myth“Poverty is a northern thing”

In headline income, the North looks poorer and the South looks richer, and that is where the mental map stops. But poverty is about what is left after the essentials, and the biggest essential is rent. Once housing costs are counted, the map redraws.

The gapThe rent does the work

One number keeps flipping the story: housing. A payslip looks fine until the rent is paid, and the official statistics measure both moments. Before housing costs, the country looks steadier; after, the middle slides down and more people fall below the line.

This is why "after housing costs" is the number that tracks how life actually feels, and why two people on the same salary can live in different worlds depending on what they pay to keep a roof up.

Why the gap matters

Misplacing yourself is not a harmless quirk. It shapes what feels fair. If everyone believes they are middling, then every tax rise sounds like it lands on "people like me", every benefit sounds like it goes to someone else, and the actual shape of the country, a bunched low-and-middle with a long rich tail, stays invisible.

  • The average is not the middle. A long tail of high earners pulls the mean above what most people make. When a number is called "average", ask whether it is the mean or the median.
  • Housing is the hinge. Almost every surprising fact here, pensioners doing well, London doing badly, comes from measuring income after the rent rather than before.
  • Your yardstick is local. We compare ourselves to the people around us, not to the country. That is what keeps almost everyone feeling average.

Perception, unlike the economy, is something we can actually correct. The numbers are public, and now you know where you stand in them.

Sources & methodology

Income is equivalised net household income, adjusted for household size to the couple-with-no-children yardstick, exactly as HBAI reports it. The calculator applies the OECD-modified companion scale (which differs before and after housing costs) and reads your percentile off the whole-population distribution; treat it as a good estimate, not a precise ranking. Relative poverty means below 60% of the contemporary median, so it moves with the median rather than being a fixed line. Head counts multiply your percentile by the UK resident population (about 68.3 million, ONS mid-2023). The published distribution collapses the very top into a single "£1,500+ a week" band, so shares are normalised per group; that is accurate at low and middle incomes but understates the extreme top. Regional figures use JRF's after-housing rates (a different period and measure from the distribution above), so read them as an indicative overlay. Figures are the FYE 2024 vintage.
